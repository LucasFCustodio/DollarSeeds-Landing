Entrepreneurship: multiplying what you've been given

By Lucas Custodio · Published August 18, 2026

Somewhere along the way, ambition got a bad reputation in church. But the Bible is full of builders, traders, and founders — and one of Jesus' most famous parables is, on its face, a story about capital and return. This lesson is about entrepreneurship as stewardship: multiplying what you've been given, for reasons bigger than yourself.

Is entrepreneurship biblical?

Yes — Scripture consistently honors work, initiative, and building, from the very first chapters. Humanity's first assignment is to cultivate and multiply what God planted (Genesis 1:28, 2:15). The Proverbs 31 woman evaluates a field, buys it, and plants a vineyard from her earnings — a real-estate deal and an agricultural startup in two verses (Proverbs 31:16). Lydia, one of the first European converts, was a dealer in purple cloth — a merchant whose business hosted a church (Acts 16:14–15). Paul funded his missionary work making tents.

What Scripture condemns is never enterprise itself — it's enterprise with no one but yourself at the end of it.

What does the parable of the talents teach about money?

In Matthew 25:14–30, a master entrusts three servants with his wealth, and the two who put it to work — doubling it — are praised, while the one who buried it in the ground to keep it safe is condemned. A talent was money: years of wages. The parable's uncomfortable math is that preserving what you were given, untouched, was the failing strategy.

Three things follow. God gives different amounts to different people, and the reward for faithfulness is identical for the five-talent and two-talent servants — you're measured against what you received. Multiplying it requires risk; burying it was the safe play and the wrong one. And the servants never stop being stewards: it was the master's wealth on day one and on settlement day. That's the frame for every side gig, investment, and business idea you'll ever have.

How do I start with what I have?

Start smaller than feels impressive, and fund it from margin you already control — "whoever is faithful with very little will also be faithful with much" (Luke 16:10).

  1. Build the margin first. A business idea funded by debt starts life owned by someone else. Your Savings bucket is seed capital — the Wealth Builder track, with its 50% savings rate, exists exactly for this.
  2. Set a named goal. In DollarSeeds, create a savings goal for the venture — the camera, the course, the first inventory — and fund it a little every week.
  3. Count the cost before you build. Jesus said no one builds a tower without first sitting down and estimating the cost (Luke 14:28–30). A budget is how you sit down.

What separates ambition from greed?

The direction the money points when it arrives. Greed multiplies to accumulate; stewardship multiplies to bless — employees paid well, a family provided for, a church supported, generosity that scales with the business.

That's the DollarSeeds conviction in one line: we don't want money just to fill our pockets, but to use it in a way that pleases God and blesses the people around us. Ambition inside that frame isn't a temptation to manage. It's a calling to fund.
